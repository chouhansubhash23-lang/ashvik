# Acute Diarrhoea (including cholera and dysentery)

> **Source:** National Health Portal; ICMR/NCDC diarrhoeal disease guidance; WHO ORS/zinc
> **Evidence:** `../../evidence/sources.md`
> **Last verified:** 2026-10-27
> **Status:** Draft — clinician review required
> **Setting:** Rural OPD / PHC (India)

## 1. Quick facts

- Leading cause of childhood death in India; dehydration is the main killer.
- Causes: rotavirus, norovirus, *E. coli*, *Shigella*, *Vibrio cholerae*, *Salmonella*, *Giardia*, *Entamoeba*.
- Most acute diarrhoea is self-limiting; treatment is **rehydration**, not antibiotics.

## 2. Clinical features

- Loose/watery stools (≥ 3 in 24 h)
- Vomiting, abdominal cramps
- Fever (some infections)
- Blood/mucus in stool → dysentery
- Rice-water stools → suspect cholera
- Dehydration signs: thirst, dry mouth, sunken eyes, reduced urine, lethargy

## 3. Dehydration assessment (WHO)

| Sign | Some dehydration | Severe dehydration |
|---|---|---|
| Condition | Restless, irritable | Lethargic, unconscious |
| Eyes | Sunken | Sunken |
| Thirst | Drinks eagerly | Drinks poorly / unable |
| Skin pinch | Goes back slowly | Goes back very slowly |
| Treatment | ORS + zinc at home/OPD | IV fluids + urgent referral |

## 4. Diagnosis

| Test | When |
|---|---|
| Stool routine/microscopy | Blood/mucus, suspected dysentery |
| Stool culture | Cholera outbreak, severe cases |
| Rapid cholera RDT | Outbreak setting |
| Electrolytes, creatinine | Severe dehydration / IV fluids |

## 5. Treatment

### 5.1 All cases — rehydration

| Measure | Detail |
|---|---|
| ORS | After every loose stool: < 2 yrs 50–100 mL; 2–10 yrs 100–200 mL; older children/adults 200–250 mL |
| Zinc (children) | < 6 months: 10 mg daily; ≥ 6 months: 20 mg daily — for **14 days** |
| Continue feeding | Breastfeeding and normal food; do not starve |
| IV fluids | Ringer's lactate if severe dehydration or unable to drink |

**Brand example (illustrative):** ORS — Electral; Zinc — Zinconia.

### 5.2 Symptomatic therapy

| Drug | Brand example (illustrative) | Adult dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Racecadotril | Redotil, Zedott | 100 mg three times daily | 3–5 days; adjunct only |
| Zinc | Zinconia | 20 mg once daily | Children: 14 days |

**Avoid loperamide in children and in bloody diarrhoea.**

### 5.3 When antibiotics are indicated

| Condition | Drug | Adult dose | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cholera (suspected/confirmed) | Doxycycline | 300 mg single dose | Once |
| Cholera (pregnancy/children) | Azithromycin | 1 g single dose (adult); 20 mg/kg (child) | Once |
| Shigellosis / dysentery | Ciprofloxacin | 500 mg twice daily | 3 days |
| Shigellosis (alternative) | Azithromycin | 500 mg once daily | 3 days |
| Amoebic dysentery | Metronidazole | 500–750 mg three times daily | 7–10 days |

**Do not give antibiotics for watery diarrhoea without cholera/dysentery features.**

## 6. Drug interactions and cautions

- **ORS:** no significant interactions; do not mix with soft drinks (high sugar worsens diarrhoea).
- **Zinc:** may reduce absorption of tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones — separate by 2 hours; can cause vomiting (give with food).
- **Racecadotril:** generally safe; caution in renal impairment.
- **Doxycycline:** avoid in pregnancy and children < 8 years (except cholera where benefit outweighs risk); photosensitivity; antacids reduce absorption.
- **Ciprofloxacin:** QT prolongation, tendon injury, avoid in children/pregnancy unless needed.
- **Metronidazole:** metallic taste, disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol; may increase INR with warfarin.

## 7. Red flags — refer immediately

- Severe dehydration / shock
- Blood in stool with toxicity
- Persistent vomiting, unable to drink
- No urine for 8–12 hours
- Sunken eyes, skin pinch very slow
- Altered consciousness, seizures
- High fever with abdominal rigidity
- Infants < 6 months, malnourished children, elderly
- Suspected cholera outbreak

## 8. Follow-up

- Review in 24–48 hours if not improving.
- Teach caregivers ORS preparation and danger signs.
- Report suspected cholera clusters to the health authority.
